Homily for Fourth Saturday of Dedication of the Church
Saturday, November 28, 2026
Readings: Rev 21:22-27; Jn 4:19-26

Dear brothers and sisters in Christ,

The Samaritan woman comes to the well for water. Before she leaves, she is talking with Jesus about where people can meet God. She points to the mountain where her ancestors worshiped, while the Jews say that Jerusalem is the place to worship. We may hear an old dispute. For her, it is a living question: Does someone from my people have a place before God? Here on the fourth Saturday of the Season of the Dedication of the Church, it is a good question to bring into our own church. When we come through these doors, what are we looking for? And what is the Father looking for in us?

Jesus does not tell the woman that the differences between their peoples were imaginary. He says plainly that salvation is from the Jews. The God who meets her at the well is the God who called Israel and remained faithful to his promises. Jesus himself comes from that people. Yet the faithfulness of God is precisely what opens the way for this woman. The promise is not a private possession. In Jesus, it reaches someone who might reasonably have expected him to keep his distance. Notice how seriously he takes her question. He does not ask her to find someone more qualified to speak for her.

Jesus says that the hour is coming, and is already here, when true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth. Already here: that is a remarkable thing to say beside a well. Nothing about the place has changed. What has changed is that Jesus is there, speaking to her. She says that when the Messiah comes, he will explain everything. Jesus does not give her another date to wait for. He tells her that the very one speaking to her is the Messiah. The answer to her question about where God can be found begins with recognizing who is sitting there with her.

What does it mean, then, to worship in spirit and truth? It means more than being sincere, though sincerity matters. We cannot make ourselves children of God by trying to feel something holy. It is the Holy Spirit who opens our hearts to the Father, and it is Jesus who shows us the truth of who the Father is. The Son brings us into his own relationship with the Father. So a person who feels tired and distracted can still worship truly. A grieving person who can only manage a few words can still pray in the Spirit. The measure is not the strength of the feeling. We are entrusting ourselves to the Father through the Son, even when that trust is all we have to offer.

And Jesus says that the Father seeks such worshipers. We often picture ourselves trying to get God's attention. The Gospel gives us a different picture. Before the woman knows whom she is talking to, Jesus has already spoken to her. Before she has resolved her questions, he makes himself known. Some of us hesitate to pray because it has been a long time, or because there is something in our lives we wish were different. Today's Gospel gives us reason to begin right there. The Father is not waiting for a polished account of our lives. He is seeking people who will let him meet them as they are and lead them into the truth.

The reading from Revelation shows us where this meeting leads. John sees the holy city and notices that it has no temple. The Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple. The Lamb, Jesus who gave himself for us and lives, is also its lamp. There is no need to reserve one part of the city for God's presence, as though the rest must make do without him. The whole city lives in his light. Its gates do not close, and the nations bring their glory into it. Yet nothing false enters. God's welcome does not mean that deceit has ceased to matter. It means that we are invited into a life in which we no longer have to hide behind it.

This helps us understand why the Church sets apart a season for the dedication of the church. If God is not confined to a building, why take such care of one? Because here we learn together to recognize the presence that will fill the holy city. We hear the Word with people we did not choose. We stand or sit beside people whose burdens we may never know. We bring real bodies, real voices, and the events of a real week. What takes place here is not an escape from ordinary life. It teaches us how to live the rest of it before God.

Think of how much of our parish life happens between languages and generations. A grandparent may know a Malayalam prayer by heart. A grandchild may need to hear in English what that prayer is asking of God. Neither should be made to feel like a stranger. It can be a gift to take a line of a prayer and speak about it together at home. The elder may have something to tell about the hope carried by those words. The younger person may ask the question that helps the whole family hear them freshly. The Lord at the well is not afraid of a sincere question, and we need not be either.

This week, try bringing one unedited sentence to God. Before the school run, or after the house has grown quiet, take a moment to say what you would otherwise keep to yourself. Perhaps it is, ‘Father, I am afraid of what comes next,’ or, ‘I am still angry, and I do not know how to let go.’ Then ask the Holy Spirit to help you stay there without rushing to excuse yourself or to solve everything. Such a prayer may last less than a minute. It can be the first honest moment of the day, and it can make the next honest moment easier.

Then let that prayer affect one conversation. If you have spoken sharply at home, go back and name what you did without adding a defense. Say, ‘I should not have spoken to you that way. I am sorry.’ If you notice someone standing alone after church, make time for a greeting and listen long enough to hear the answer. We cannot open the gates of the heavenly city by our own efforts. But we can stop shutting the small doors that have been entrusted to us. Sometimes what makes the Father's welcome believable to another person is that someone in his house has made room to listen.

In a few moments, the Holy Qurbana will bring us to the heart of this Gospel. We give thanks to the Father through the Son and invoke the Holy Spirit upon the gifts. Christ gives himself to us; we do not produce his presence by the intensity of our devotion. Bring to the altar one thing from the week that you have not yet put into God's hands. It might be a decision, a memory that still hurts, or a joy you have been too busy to acknowledge. You do not have to find impressive words. In the offering of Christ, there is room for your poor and honest offering. And in receiving him, we ask to become a people whose life together shows something of the One we have received.

When we leave, the demands of the day may be just what they were when we arrived. A difficulty at home may still need patience. A question about the future may remain unanswered. But remember how quietly this Gospel turns. The place is still a well. The woman is still a Samaritan. Yet she is no longer only waiting for someone who might come one day. The Lord is speaking to her now. Let us carry that confidence home. The Father who sought her seeks us. May the Spirit give us the courage to be truthful before him, and may the Lamb light the way from this altar to the very next person who needs our patience, our attention, or our love. Amen.
