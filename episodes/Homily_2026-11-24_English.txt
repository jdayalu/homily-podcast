Homily for the Fourth Tuesday of Dedication of the Church
Tuesday, November 24, 2026
Readings: 1 Peter 2:1-10; John 10:7-16

Dear brothers and sisters in Christ,

We are now in the final weeks of our liturgical year. The Church calls this season the Season of the Dedication of the Church. In a few days we will begin the Season of Annunciation, and we will start to prepare for Christmas. But before we turn that page, the Church asks us to stop and look at ourselves. Who are we as the Church? What does it mean to belong to Christ? And who is leading us?

Today's readings give us a beautiful answer. The Gospel tells us that Jesus is the gate and the Good Shepherd. And Saint Peter tells us that we are living stones, built together into a spiritual house. Let us reflect on these two images together.

First, the Gospel. Jesus says something very simple and very strong. He says, "I am the gate." Think about what a gate does. A gate is the way in. It is the way to safety. In the time of Jesus, shepherds kept their sheep in a stone enclosure at night. There was only one opening. And often the shepherd himself would lie down across that opening. He became the gate.

So when Jesus says, "I am the gate," he is saying, "I place my own body between you and danger." That is exactly what he did on the Cross. He lay down his life across the doorway, so that we could be safe.

Jesus also says that whoever enters through him will be saved, will come in and go out, and will find pasture. Notice that. He does not say we will be locked inside. We come in, and we go out. Life with Jesus is not a prison. It is freedom. We come in to rest, to pray, to be fed. And then we go out into the world, to our families, our jobs, our schools. And wherever we go, we find pasture, because he goes with us.

Then Jesus contrasts himself with the thief. The thief comes only to steal, to kill, and to destroy. But Jesus came so that we may have life, and have it to the full.

Brothers and sisters, there are many thieves in our world today. Sometimes the thief is the phone that steals our evening and our sleep. Sometimes the thief is anxiety that steals our peace. Sometimes it is a bitterness that steals our joy, or a habit that slowly steals our freedom. Sometimes it is a voice that tells our young people that their worth depends on how many likes they get. These thieves promise life, but they take life away. Jesus is different. He never takes from us. He only gives.

Next, Jesus says, "I am the Good Shepherd." And he tells us what makes him good. The Good Shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. The hired hand, when he sees the wolf coming, runs away, because the sheep do not belong to him. But Jesus does not run. We belong to him. He knows us, and we know him, just as the Father knows him and he knows the Father.

Think about that for a moment. Jesus knows you. Not just your name. He knows your worries about your children. He knows the pain you carry from the past. He knows the struggle of the student who feels lost, and the loneliness of the elder whose family lives far away. And he does not run away.

Many of us in this parish came to this country from far away. Some came as students, some as nurses, some as engineers, some as young families. Sometimes we felt like sheep in a strange land. But the Good Shepherd came with us. He gathered us here, into this parish, into this flock. That is his work, not ours.

And then Jesus says something very important. He says he has other sheep that do not belong to this fold. He must bring them also. They will listen to his voice, and there will be one flock and one shepherd.

One flock, one shepherd. This is the dream of Jesus for his Church. Not many small groups pulling in different directions. One flock. In our parish we have many people. Different ages, different backgrounds, different opinions. Some of us pray best in Malayalam, some in English. That is not a problem. That is a gift. But it becomes a problem if we forget that we belong to one shepherd. Our unity does not come from agreeing on everything. It comes from listening to the same voice.

Now let us turn to our first reading. Saint Peter writes to Christians who were scattered and often treated as outsiders. He tells them to put away all malice, all deceit, all insincerity, envy, and slander. Then he says, like newborn babies, long for pure spiritual milk, so that you may grow.

And then he gives this beautiful image. Come to Christ, the living stone, rejected by people but chosen and precious to God. And like living stones, let yourselves be built into a spiritual house.

This is the heart of the Season of the Dedication of the Church. When we dedicate a church building, we bless the stones and the altar. But Saint Peter reminds us that the true Church is not made only of concrete and wood. The true Church is made of people. You are the living stones. Each one of you.

Now, a stone in a wall does not stand alone. It holds up the stones above it. It rests on the stones below it. If one stone is missing, the wall is weaker. In the same way, the Church needs every one of you. The elder who prays the rosary every evening is holding up the wall. The teenager who serves at the altar is holding up the wall. The mother who teaches catechism on Sunday is holding up the wall. No one is too small to matter in God's house.

And then Saint Peter says these wonderful words. You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own people. Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people. Once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy.

Brothers and sisters, this is our identity. Before we are engineers or doctors or students or retirees, we are God's own people. We have received mercy. And our task, Peter says, is to proclaim the mighty acts of the one who called us out of darkness into his wonderful light.

So how do we live this out this week? Let me offer three simple steps.

First, listen for the Shepherd's voice each day. Take ten minutes, perhaps in the morning before the house wakes up, or at night before sleep. Open the Gospel. Read a few lines slowly. Then sit quietly and let Jesus speak. Sheep learn their shepherd's voice by hearing it every day. We must do the same.

Second, name one thief in your life and close the gate on it. Maybe it is the phone at the dinner table. Maybe it is gossip. Maybe it is a grudge you have held too long. Choose one this week. Ask Jesus to stand at the gate for you. And then make one practical change.

Third, be a living stone for someone else. Look around this parish. Is there an elder who lives alone? A new family who does not know anyone yet? A student far from home? This week, reach out to one person. A phone call. An invitation to your home for a cup of tea. A ride to church. That is how the spiritual house is built, one stone at a time.

Finally, let us remember where the Shepherd feeds his flock most fully. It is here, at this altar, in the Holy Qurbana. Here the Good Shepherd lays down his life for us again, in a sacramental way. Here he gives us not just pasture, but his own Body and Blood. Here the many living stones become one Body. When we come forward to receive Holy Communion, we are coming through the gate. And when we go out from this church, we carry the Shepherd with us into our homes and our workplaces.

As this liturgical year comes to its close, let us thank God for gathering us into one flock. Let us ask him to make us strong living stones in his house. And let us follow the voice of the Good Shepherd, who knows us, who loves us, and who will never run away.

Amen.
