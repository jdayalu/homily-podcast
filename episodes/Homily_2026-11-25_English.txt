Homily for Fourth Wednesday of Dedication of the Church
Wednesday, November 25, 2026
Readings: Ephesians 1:3-12; Luke 1:46-55

Dear brothers and sisters in Christ,

Tomorrow, across this country, families will gather around tables. Tomorrow is Thanksgiving. And today, on the eve of that day, the Church places in our hands the greatest song of thanksgiving ever sung by a human voice. It is the song of Mary, the Magnificat.

We are in the season of the Dedication of the Church, the last season of our liturgical year. In a few days we will begin the season of Annunciation, and we will wait again for the birth of our Lord. This season of Dedication asks us to look at the Church as the Bride of Christ, made holy, made beautiful, and finally brought home to the wedding feast of heaven. It is a season of looking at the finish line. Where is all of this going? What is God doing with my life, with my family, with the whole world?

Today our two readings answer that question. Saint Paul gives us the big plan. Mary gives us the song that comes from living inside that plan.

Let us begin with the Gospel.

Mary has just travelled to the hill country to visit her cousin Elizabeth. She is young. She is newly pregnant, and she carries a secret that no one in her village would understand. When Elizabeth greets her, the child in Elizabeth's womb leaps for joy. And Mary does not answer with a speech about herself. She bursts into praise. My soul proclaims the greatness of the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Saviour.

Notice where she starts. Not with her problems. Not with her plans. She starts with God. Her soul makes God great. That is the first lesson of the Magnificat. Gratitude begins when we stop making ourselves the centre and let God be big again.

Then Mary says something surprising. God has looked upon the lowliness of his servant. Mary knows she is small. She is a poor girl from an unimportant town. And yet she says that from now on every generation will call her blessed. She is not saying, look at me. She is saying, look at what God has done to someone like me. The Mighty One has done great things for me, and holy is his name.

Brothers and sisters, this is the secret of a thankful heart. Humility and joy are not enemies. They are sisters. The proud person is rarely grateful, because the proud person thinks he deserves everything. The humble person is always grateful, because she knows that everything is a gift.

Then Mary's song widens. It moves from her own story to the story of the whole world. God's mercy reaches from age to age for those who fear him. He has scattered the proud. He has brought down the powerful from their thrones and lifted up the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things and sent the rich away empty. He has come to the help of his servant Israel, remembering his promise to Abraham and his children for ever.

These are strong words. Mary sings as though the victory has already happened. The proud are scattered. The hungry are filled. But look around. In her time, Rome still ruled. The poor were still poor. So why does she sing in the past tense? Because Mary sees with the eyes of faith. She knows that in her womb God has already begun the great reversal. Once God has entered the world in Jesus, the end of the story is certain. The song of thanksgiving is sung before the full harvest arrives.

And this is exactly what Saint Paul tells us in the letter to the Ephesians. Listen to how he begins. Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing. Paul is writing from prison. He has chains on his wrists. And still, his first word is blessing.

Paul tells us that God chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world, to be holy and blameless before him in love. Think about that. Before the mountains of Kerala, before the oceans, before the first sunrise, God had you in mind. You are not an accident. Your life is not a mistake. God destined us, Paul says, to be his adopted sons and daughters through Jesus Christ. In Christ we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our sins, according to the riches of his grace, which he lavished upon us.

That word lavished matters. God is not stingy. He pours it out.

And then Paul tells us the goal of everything. God has made known to us the mystery of his will, his plan for the fullness of time: to gather up all things in Christ, things in heaven and things on earth. That is the finish line of this season of Dedication. Everything broken will be gathered. Everything scattered will be brought home.

So Mary and Paul are singing the same song. Paul gives us the blueprint. Mary gives us the music. Paul says, God chose the lowly and lavished grace on them. Mary says, he looked upon my lowliness and did great things for me. Paul says, God will gather up all things in Christ. Mary says, he remembered his promise from age to age.

Now, what does this mean for us, here, this week?

Many of us came to this country carrying very little. Some of our parents arrived with two suitcases and a Bible. Some of you are students far from home, with your families on the other side of the world. Some of you are elders who remember the paddy fields and the parish feasts back home, and you miss them. And some of you, our young people, were born here, and you are still figuring out where you belong.

To all of us, Mary says: God has looked on your lowliness. He has not forgotten you. And to all of us, Paul says: you were chosen before the world began. You belong. You are adopted into the family of God.

So let me offer three simple steps for this week.

First, before you eat tomorrow, pray the Magnificat together as a family. It is short. You can find it in Luke, chapter one. Let one of the children read it aloud, or let the grandparents lead it in Malayalam. Before the food, before the football, let your home make God great.

Second, make a list of five blessings, and make one of them a hard blessing. It is easy to thank God for good health or a good job. But Paul thanked God from a prison cell. Is there a struggle in your life where God has been quietly at work? A sickness that brought your family closer? A failure that taught you humility? Name it, and thank God for it. That is how Mary's kind of faith grows in us.

Third, be part of God's great reversal. Mary sings that God fills the hungry with good things. Very often, he does that through us. This week, fill one hungry person. It may be a neighbour who is alone for the holiday. It may be an international student who has nowhere to go tomorrow. Invite one person to your table. When we lift up the lowly, we sing the Magnificat with our hands.

Finally, brothers and sisters, remember that the word Eucharist itself means thanksgiving. Every time we celebrate the Holy Qurbana, we are doing what Mary did. We lift up our hearts. We give thanks to the Lord our God. We remember his promises, and we receive the One whom Mary carried in her womb. At this altar, the hungry are filled with good things. At this altar, the lowly are lifted up. At this altar, God gathers his scattered children into one body in Christ.

So when you receive Holy Communion today, carry Jesus as Mary did. Let your soul magnify the Lord. Then go out and bring that joy, like Mary hurrying to Elizabeth, into your homes, your workplaces, your campuses, and your Thanksgiving tables.

As this liturgical year comes to its close, let us look back with gratitude and look forward with hope. The God who began a good work in Mary is finishing his good work in his Church, and in each one of us.

May Mary, our Mother, teach us to sing her song. May we give thanks in every season, in plenty and in want. And may our whole lives become a Magnificat, to the praise of his glory.

Amen.
