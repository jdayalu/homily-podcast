Homily for Fourth Thursday of Dedication of the Church
Thursday, November 26, 2026
Readings: Heb 13:9-16; Lk 10:17-21

Dear brothers and sisters in Christ,

When someone comes home after doing something difficult, it is natural to ask, “How did it go?” We ask it after an exam, a job interview, or a visit to the doctor. We ask it after a parish program that took weeks to prepare. And when the answer is good, we want to hear all about it. Today the disciples return to Jesus with just that kind of excitement. They have seen suffering people set free. They can hardly wait to tell him. Jesus listens to their joy, and then he gives them a reason to rejoice that will still be there on the day when they come home with no success to report.

On this Fourth Thursday of the Season of the Dedication of the Church, we hear him say, “Rejoice that your names are written in heaven.” He does not tell the disciples that their work was unimportant. Earlier in Luke’s Gospel, he sent them ahead of him to the places he intended to visit. They went to bring peace, care for the sick, and announce that the kingdom of God had drawn near. Now they have seen what his name can do. They say, “Even the demons are subject to us in your name!” That last phrase matters. The authority was given to them. It was not something they had learned to produce or something they now owned.

Jesus sees more in their mission than the disciples can see. His vivid words about Satan falling like lightning show what is at stake when the kingdom draws near: the power of evil is being broken. His promise of protection is not an invitation to court danger or a guarantee that faithful people will never suffer. The other reading today takes us straight to Jesus suffering outside the gate. We understand his promise in the light of that cross. The enemy can wound; the enemy does not have the final word. Jesus can be trusted even where there is no quick rescue and no visible victory.

Then comes that little word that turns the whole conversation: “Nevertheless.” The disciples have every reason to be glad for the people who were set free. But if their deepest joy depends on having something impressive to report, what will happen when a village refuses to welcome them? What will happen when prayer seems to change nothing? Jesus gives them a joy that can survive those days. Their names are written in heaven. God knows them. They belong to him. It is not a mark they have earned for a good day’s work. It is a gift that teaches them how to live and serve on good days and bad ones alike.

Many of us know how quickly we start to measure our worth by the last thing that happened. A student opens a grade and feels that one letter has told the whole story. Someone who has spent years building a career hears that a position is being cut and wonders how to face the family. An older person who once hosted everyone now needs help to get to church, and finds it hard to ask. Even in parish life, we can come to need the next thank-you more than we realize. To each of us Jesus says: what you can do matters, but it is not the measure of who you are to the Father.

Notice what happens next. Jesus himself rejoices in the Holy Spirit. He thanks the Father for revealing these things to little children. He is not saying that thought and learning are bad. A child can receive something that a person who needs to be in control may refuse: a gift that cannot be claimed as a right. We see the Son rejoicing in the Spirit and speaking to the Father. The joy Jesus wants for his disciples has its home there, in his own relationship with the Father. We begin to taste it when we can say, simply and honestly, “Father, thank you. I could not have given this to myself.”

The Letter to the Hebrews says something very close to this: it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace. The writer will not have the community put its trust in disputes about food. Instead, he leads them to Jesus. He recalls the offerings whose blood was brought into the holy place while their remains were taken outside the camp. Then he points to Jesus, who suffered outside the city gate to sanctify his people. The place that looked like the place of rejection is precisely where we are told to go to meet him. We are to go out to him, even when it costs us some of the approval we would rather keep.

That is why Hebrews says we have no enduring city here. It does not mean our homes and neighborhoods are disposable. The same passage tells us to do good and share what we have. If we are seeking the city to come, then we can care for this place without making an idol of our place in it. We can stop guarding our standing long enough to notice who is standing alone. During the Season of the Dedication of the Church, this is worth remembering. We rightly cherish the church where our children are baptized and where we bring our grief and gratitude. But the Lord we meet here also waits for us outside the gate. The building serves its holy purpose when the people who worship inside learn to recognize him outside.

Here are three small ways to live this word. The first is a prayer before the day starts making demands. Before you check the results, the messages, or the news, make the sign of the cross and say, “Father, help me live today as someone you know and love.” You can say it on a morning you are looking forward to. Say it also on the morning you are dreading. The circumstances may be different. The One to whom you are speaking has not changed.

The second is to let thanksgiving have a place in the way you talk at home. At supper or before bed, instead of asking only what everyone got done, ask, “What can we thank God for today?” Give people time to answer. On a hard day, it might be the friend who called, or the grace to apologize after an argument. Hebrews calls praise a sacrifice. Sometimes it costs us something to make room for gratitude, especially when we would rather go over everything that went wrong. We do not have to pretend the day was easy in order to thank God for a good thing in it.

The third is to share something that will actually help. If someone has stopped coming because driving after dark is difficult, offer a ride and arrange the time. If a student far from home is spending the weekend alone, invite them to eat with you. Or call someone who is caring for an ill parent and ask when you can take over an errand. Hebrews puts sharing alongside the praise of our lips. It matters that we bless God aloud. It also matters that someone can see what that blessing means by what we are willing to give.

In the Holy Qurbana, we do not come to show the Father how well our mission has gone. We come through Jesus, who offered himself outside the gate and now gathers us at his table. We give thanks to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit. When we bring bread and wine to the altar, we can also bring the work no one saw, the joy we hardly know how to express, and the trouble we cannot yet put right. None of it has to be hidden. The Lord who gives himself to us knows the people he is feeding. And he sends us back to serve without having to prove that we deserve a place at his table.

So, when the day is over and someone asks, “How did it go?” tell the truth. Give thanks for what went well. Ask for help where you need it. And remember what Jesus told his friends when they came home to him: rejoice that your names are written in heaven. Let that be the joy you carry into tomorrow. Amen.
