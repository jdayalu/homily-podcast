Homily for the Fourth Friday of Dedication of the Church
Friday, November 27, 2026
Readings: Revelation 7:9-12; Mark 12:18-27

Dear brothers and sisters in Christ,

Yesterday many of us sat around a full table. We gave thanks. We saw faces we love, and some of us felt the empty chair of someone who is no longer with us. Perhaps a grandmother who always made the payasam. Perhaps a father who used to lead the family prayer. On a weekend of thanksgiving, our hearts hold both joy and longing at the same time.

Today's Gospel speaks directly to that longing.

We are in the final days of the season of the Dedication of the Church, Pallikoodasha. This is the last season of our liturgical year. In these weeks the Church lifts our eyes to the end of all things, to the heavenly Jerusalem, to the Church made perfect in glory. And this Sunday, we begin a new year with the season of Annunciation, as we prepare for the birth of our Lord. So today is a good day to ask a simple question. Where is all of this going? Where is my life going? Where are the people I love going?

In the Gospel of Mark, some Sadducees come to Jesus. The Sadducees did not believe in the resurrection of the dead. So they bring Him a clever puzzle. A woman marries a man. He dies without children. By the law of Moses, his brother marries her. He also dies. Then the next brother, and the next, until seven brothers have married her. Finally the woman dies too. And they ask Jesus, with a little smile perhaps: in the resurrection, whose wife will she be?

It is a trick question. They are not really looking for the truth. They want to show that the resurrection is foolish, that it does not make sense.

Notice how Jesus answers. He does not mock them. But He is very direct. He tells them they are mistaken, because they know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God.

Brothers and sisters, that is a sentence worth remembering. You are wrong because you do not know the Scriptures, and you do not know the power of God.

The Sadducees imagined heaven as simply a longer version of this life. The same arrangements, the same problems, the same arguments, just going on forever. And so the resurrection looked ridiculous to them.

But Jesus says that when the dead rise, they do not marry or are given in marriage. They are like the angels in heaven. He is not saying that our love for one another is erased. He is saying that the life to come is not a copy of this life. It is something new. It is fuller, deeper, and more beautiful than anything we can picture. Our human categories are too small for what God has prepared.

Then Jesus goes further. He takes them back to Moses at the burning bush. There God calls Himself the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Not "I was their God." But "I am their God." Abraham, Isaac and Jacob had died centuries before. Yet God speaks of them as living. And Jesus concludes with these powerful words: He is not God of the dead, but of the living.

Let that sink into your heart today. Our God is the God of the living.

The people we love who have died in Christ are not lost. They are not simply memories in a photo album. They are alive in God. The grandmother who prayed the rosary every evening, the father who taught you to make the sign of the cross, the friend who died too young. If they belong to Christ, they are held in the hands of the God of the living.

Now look at our first reading from the Book of Revelation. Saint John is given a vision. He sees a great crowd that no one can count. People from every nation, every tribe, every people and every language. They stand before the throne and before the Lamb. They wear white robes. They hold palm branches in their hands. And they cry out that salvation belongs to our God and to the Lamb. And all the angels fall on their faces and worship, saying blessing and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving and honour belong to our God forever.

Brothers and sisters, this is the answer to the Sadducees. This is where the story ends. Not in an empty grave of hopelessness, but in a great multitude singing before the throne of God.

And did you notice? Every nation, every tribe, every language. That includes Malayalam. That includes English. That includes the grandparents who came from Kerala and the children who were born here in America. Our parish itself is a small picture of that great crowd. People from different places, different generations, different languages, gathered around one altar, before one Lamb.

So what does this mean for us this week? Let me offer three simple steps.

First, know the Scriptures. Jesus said the Sadducees were wrong because they did not know the Scriptures. Many of us know the Bible stories from childhood, but we have not opened the Bible in a long time. This week, take ten minutes each day with the Gospel of the day. Read it slowly. Read it with your family if you can. Parents, read a short passage with your children before bed. Young people, there are good Bible apps on your phones. Let the Word of God become familiar again, like the voice of a friend.

Second, pray for your beloved dead, and talk with your family about them. We live in a culture that does not like to speak about death. But we are people of the resurrection. This weekend, while the family is still together after Thanksgiving, share a story about a grandparent or a relative who has gone before us. Light a candle. Pray together for them. Have a Qurbana offered for their souls. When we pray for our dead, we are saying with Jesus that God is the God of the living.

Third, live now as citizens of heaven. If our future is that great crowd before the throne, then our life today should already sound like their song. They sing of blessing and glory and thanksgiving. So let thanksgiving not end yesterday. This week, thank God each morning before you look at your phone. Thank the people in your home. Forgive someone you have been holding something against. Heaven is a place of praise and of peace, and we can begin practising it here, in our homes, in our workplaces, in our schools.

The Sadducees thought the resurrection was too strange to believe. But for us, the resurrection is not a puzzle. It is a promise. And it is a person. Jesus Christ is risen from the dead, and He is the firstborn of many brothers and sisters.

Dear friends, every time we gather for the Holy Qurbana, we stand at the doorway of that heavenly vision. When the priest invites us to lift up our hearts, and we join the angels in singing Holy, Holy, Holy, we are not alone. We join the great crowd from every nation. We join the angels who bow before the throne. We join our own beloved dead who are alive in God. The Qurbana is where heaven and earth meet. It is where the Lamb who was slain gives Himself to us as food for the journey. The Body and Blood of Christ that we receive is the seed of our own resurrection. Jesus Himself promised that whoever eats His flesh and drinks His blood has eternal life, and He will raise them up on the last day.

So when you come forward to receive Holy Communion, think of those you love who have gone before you. They are closer than you think. They are at the same altar, on the other side of the veil.

As this liturgical year comes to its close, and as we prepare to begin the season of Annunciation on Sunday, let us renew our faith in the God of the living. Let us know the Scriptures. Let us trust the power of God. And let us live today with the joy of people who know where the story ends: in the presence of God, with a great crowd that no one can count, singing blessing and glory and thanksgiving forever.

May Mother Mary, who now shares fully in the glory of her Son, pray for us and for all our beloved dead.

Amen.
